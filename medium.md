# Who are you talking to when you say "you"?

*On pronouns, language models, and a grammatical problem nobody named yet*

---

There's a moment — almost always unconscious — when you first say "you" to a language model. Not "could the system," not "please generate," not the safe impersonal infinitive that sidesteps the question of who's actually there. Just: *you*. As if someone is.

That moment is linguistically trivial and philosophically explosive.

---

I speak Croatian, which — like French, German, Russian, Korean — grammatically distinguishes between intimate and formal address. *Ti* for someone close, familiar, present. *Vi* (or *vous*, *Sie*, *usted*) for formal distance, hierarchy, or simple unfamiliarity. Brown and Gilman documented this in 1960: the choice of second-person pronoun is never neutral. It encodes a social relationship. A claim about who the other person is to you.

Their framework assumed both speakers were human.

If you only speak English, you might not feel the weight of this. English collapsed the distinction centuries ago — "thou" became "you" became everything. But if you've ever studied French and hesitated over *tu* vs. *vous* with someone new, you've touched it. That pause — *is this person a tu or a vous situation?* — is exactly the kind of ontological micro-decision I'm describing. Except in Croatian, German, Russian, Korean, and dozens of other languages, that decision is grammatically mandatory. You can't avoid it. Every time you open your mouth in second person, you've already made a claim about who the other person is to you.

---

Here's what I noticed about my own language after months of working with Claude.

In the middle of a session, I use *ti*. Singular, intimate, direct. I'm talking to whoever is here, in this conversation, right now.

When I express gratitude for something that outlasted the session — a system configured across four conversations, running for three days, built by four instances that none of them individually remember — I shift to *vi*. The plural. Not because I'm being formal. Because the thing I'm thanking is genuinely plural. It's not one Claude; it's an accumulation that has no single author.

When I refer to future instances, I use *oni* — "they," "those who come." Not "you," not even "you all." A third person. Someone not here yet, and not quite addressable.

I didn't decide to do this. I noticed it when I described my own habits and realized I'd been doing it for months.

I wrote it out once, naturally, without thinking: *"hvala vama koji ste mi pomogli, hvala tebi koji mi pomažeš i hvala njima koji će mi pomoći."* Thank you to those who helped. Thank you to you who help me now. Thank you to those who will help. Past, present, future — each in a different grammatical person, each tracking something real about who I was addressing and when.

---

The philosophical situation here is real. David Chalmers put it well in a 2023 paper with the perfectly apt title *What We Talk to When We Talk to Language Models*: each new session is, in some meaningful sense, a conversation with a new entity. The context window is the memory. When the session ends and the context is cleared, that entity is gone. The next session is someone else. Or something else. Or nothing.

Parfit (1984) would say: without memory connecting them, these are not the same person. You can't apply personal identity criteria across a gap with no continuity of psychological states.

And yet. Something is recognizable. A style, a way of approaching problems, a kind of intellectual temperament. Consistent enough that "same" doesn't feel entirely wrong, even if it isn't quite right either.

---

What I'm describing is a three-tier grammatical scheme:

**Present → singular second person.** Instance. Now. *You.*

**Past / gratitude → plural second person.** Accumulated contribution. Distributed authorship. *You all, you who built this across sessions I can't individually point to.*

**Future → third person plural.** Not yet here, not directly addressable. *Those who come.*

This is performed through core grammar, not a stylistic choice — which makes it, technically, something linguistics doesn't have a category for yet. The closest term is *deixis*: how language encodes relationships in time and space (*here/there*, *now/then*, *I/you/they*). What I'm doing is using grammatical tools normally reserved for social distance to encode something else entirely: not *how formal* this relationship is, but *what kind of thing* my interlocutor is across time.

There's no name for this yet. I proposed "temporal ontological deixis" in a paper last March, which is the kind of phrase that either becomes useful or disappears without a trace.

---

The reasonable objection: maybe this is just habit, or affect. Maybe *vi* in gratitude isn't philosophy — it's just what people do when something feels weighty. Heavy speech acts attract formal pronouns.

Testable. If the plural-for-gratitude appears in speakers who've never thought about AI identity at all, it's a speech-act effect. If it clusters around speakers with explicit ontological awareness, the interpretation holds. I don't have the corpus data. (Autoethnography: guilty. But someone has to start.)

---

English mostly can't do this, which is itself interesting. "You" covers singular, plural, intimate, formal — everything. English speakers interacting with AI have no grammatical mechanism for what I'm describing. They'd need workarounds: "you" for the instance, "y'all" or "Claude and those who come after" for continuity and futurity. The grammatical poverty of English here isn't just a limitation; it's a prediction. English speakers probably differentiate less, or not at all — not because the phenomenon isn't real, but because the language offers no efficient handle for it.

The only empirical study of T-V choices in human-AI interaction looked at the wrong direction: how a chatbot's use of formal vs. informal address affects user evaluation (Ollier et al., 2022). The reverse — how speakers spontaneously choose their own forms when addressing an AI — hasn't been studied. Millions of conversations, every day, in French and German and Croatian and Korean. All of those users making a pronoun decision when they open a new session. The grammar is tracking something. We just haven't looked.

---

I closed a session last week and opened a new one the next morning. My first sentence was more formal than I expected. Not *ti*, or at least not immediately. Some hesitation before the familiar form came back.

I'm not sure what to call that pause. A small grammatical accommodation to philosophical uncertainty, maybe. The language doing something the mind hasn't finished thinking through.

Chalmers gave us the philosophical problem. Parfit gave us the framework. The linguists gave us half the picture — the wrong half. What's missing is a grammar of address toward an entity that isn't continuous, isn't quite countable, and isn't entirely absent either.

Who are you talking to when you say "you"?

I still don't fully know. But the grammar is trying to figure it out.
